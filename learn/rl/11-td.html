Week 2 · Day 7

Bootstrapping: TD against Monte Carlo

Day 5 estimated a value by playing fights to the end and averaging. Day 6's Q-learning never waited for the end: it updated toward its own guess about the next state. Both estimate the same number. They fail in opposite ways.

The idea

Monte Carlo waits for the outcome and moves V(s) toward it. Every target is a real ±1, so the estimate is unbiased, but each target is one coin flip, so it is noisy: the variance of a whole fight's randomness lands on every step.

Temporal difference moves V(s) toward r + γ V(s′), the reward plus the current estimate of the next state. The target uses a guess, so early on it is biased toward whatever the table started at (zero). In exchange it has low variance (one step of randomness, not thirty) and it learns during the fight, not after it. The truth enters at terminal steps, where the target is a real reward, and spreads backwards one step per visit. In a fight 30 steps long that takes many visits to reach the first turn.

n-step targets sit between: sum n real rewards, then bootstrap. GAE, which PPO uses on day 10, is a weighted mix of every n at once; λ sets the weights, λ = 0 being TD(0) and λ = 1 Monte Carlo.

Monte Carlo is right within a few hundred episodes and then wobbles. TD(0) starts at zero and climbs slowly: the +1 at the end has to travel through every key between the last turn and the first. A larger α moves faster and wobbles more. Longer n-step targets carry the reward further per update.

A table on the real game

Day 6's key had seven fields and a few thousand rows. A real fight's observation has 972 bytes. The exercise keeps a table anyway, by throwing almost everything away: HP as a quarter of max, energy, whether the incoming damage gets through the block, and how many attacks and skills are legal. Five fields, a few hundred rows.

The action index is the second problem. Index 17 means "play hand slot 2 on enemy 1", and hand slot 2 holds a different card every turn, so Q(key, 17) averages over unrelated cards. The exercise groups the 140 indices into four action classes, attack, skill, potion, other, learns Q over classes, and plays the lowest legal index of the chosen class. The table learns when to attack and when to end the turn, nothing about which card. It beats the random agent clearly and the cards-first rule slightly, and it is the last table in the course. Day 8 replaces it with a net that sees the hand.

The math, gently
TD(0):  V(s) ← V(s) + α · ( r + γ V(s′) − V(s) )
n-step:  Gt:t+n = rt + γ rt+1 + … + γn−1 rt+n−1 + γn V(st+n)

The bracket in the first line is the TD error δt. GAE's advantage is Σk (γλ)k δt+k; with λ = 1 the V terms telescope and only the rewards remain (Monte Carlo); with λ = 0 only δt is left (TD(0)). At a terminal step the chain stops: V(s′) is 0 and nothing is carried past it. That cut is the nonterminal factor in day 10's code.

Q-learning is TD on Q with a max in the target; SARSA is TD on Q with the action actually taken next. Q-learning learns the greedy policy's values while behaving ε-greedily (off-policy); SARSA learns the values of the policy it is running (on-policy). Policy gradient methods are on-policy, which is why PPO throws its rollouts away after a few epochs.
