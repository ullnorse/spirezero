Week 2 · Day 9

Policy gradients and PPO: push up what worked

Q-learning learns values and derives the policy from them. There is a more direct route: keep a probability for each action and, after each fight, raise the probabilities of the moves you made if you won and lower them if you lost. That is a policy gradient. PPO is the version of it that works reliably at scale, and it is what you will run on SpireZero.

The idea

The agent's policy is a set of probabilities, π(a | s), one per legal action per state. Play a fight by sampling from them. At the end you know the return G (+1 or −1). The update is almost embarrassingly simple:

For every (state, action) in the fight: nudge π(action | state) up if G was good, down if G was bad.

Three problems show up immediately, and each has a fix with a name:

  • Credit assignment. You won, but was it because of the Defend on turn 2 or in spite of it? The raw update blames every move equally. The fix is to compare each move's outcome against what you expected from that state: the advantage, A = G − V(s). A win from a state worth +0.9 teaches almost nothing; a win from a state worth −0.5 teaches a lot. V comes from a learned critic (day 5's value, day 8's net).
  • Noise. One fight's outcome is a coin flip. Average over many fights per update.
  • Overshooting. A big step on a noisy estimate can wreck a policy that took hours to learn, and then the fights it plays next are garbage, so the data is garbage. PPO's fix: clip how far any one update may move an action's probability.

Compared with Q-learning this is slower and noisier, and in the toy that's all you see. Its advantages appear at scale: it handles large and masked action spaces naturally (just zero out the illegal probabilities), it can learn stochastic policies, and the update is a plain gradient step on a neural net, which is exactly what deep learning tooling is good at.

PPO, in pictures

Proximal Policy Optimization is a policy gradient with a seatbelt. For each action taken, compute the ratio between how likely the new policy makes it and how likely the old one did. The update wants ratio > 1 for good actions (advantage positive) and < 1 for bad ones. PPO says: once the ratio has moved past 1 ± ε in the direction the advantage wants, stop pushing. Drag the slider.

Toy · the clipped objective

x: ratio πnew(a|s) / πold(a|s). y: the objective being maximised. Where the curve is flat, the gradient is zero and the update has no more effect: the policy can't run away on one batch of data.

PPO, in words: the five parts of one training iteration

  1. Collect. Run the current policy in N parallel envs for T steps each (say 256 envs × 128 steps = 32,768 transitions). Store observation, mask, action, the action's log-probability under the policy, the reward, done, and the critic's V(s).
  2. Advantages. For each transition compute A = (what actually happened afterwards) − V(s). In practice "what happened afterwards" is a blend of the real rewards for a few steps and the critic's estimate beyond (GAE, with a knob λ between "trust the critic" and "trust the rollout"). Normalise the batch of advantages to mean 0, std 1.
  3. Update the policy. Several passes (epochs) over the batch in minibatches, maximising the clipped objective above. Illegal actions are masked out of the softmax before anything is computed.
  4. Update the critic. Regress V(s) toward the observed returns (mean squared error). Usually the policy and critic share a body and are trained together with the losses added.
  5. Entropy bonus. Add a small reward for keeping the policy's probabilities spread out, so it doesn't collapse onto one action before it has explored. This is PPO's version of day 4's ε.

Then throw the batch away (the policy has changed; the data is stale) and go back to step 1. That last sentence is why env speed matters so much for PPO: it is on-policy, every iteration needs fresh fights, and the number of fights it can afford per hour is set by the environment's step rate.

Where this lives in SpireZero
  • python/sts2/env.py: Sts2PufferEnv is the N-parallel-envs object step 1 needs, with action_masks for step 3 and the per-env reward and terminal flags for step 2. Its step auto-resets finished fights, so a rollout buffer sees continuous data.
  • PufferLib (pufferlib.PufferEnv, optional in the binding) ships a PPO trainer that consumes exactly this interface. Writing a small PPO yourself first (day 11) is the better way to learn it.
  • The on-policy data appetite is the reason behind docs/specs/rl-2x-plan.md: the whole 2× speed programme exists so step 1 is cheap.
The math, gently

Let the policy have parameters θ. The policy gradient theorem says the gradient of expected return is

∇θ J = E[ Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · At ]

Read: move each taken action's log-probability in proportion to its advantage. REINFORCE uses At = Gt − b with a constant baseline b; actor-critic uses At = Gt − V(st). For a tabular softmax, ∂ log π(a)/∂zb = 1[a = b] − π(b), which is the update the toy applies.

PPO's clipped surrogate, with rt(θ) = πθ(at|st) / πold(at|st):

LCLIP = E[ min( rt At, clip(rt, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) At ) ]

The full loss is −LCLIP + c1 · (V(st) − Gt)2 − c2 · H[π(·|st)], with typical c1 = 0.5, c2 = 0.01, ε = 0.2. GAE: At = Σk (γλ)k δt+k with δt = rt + γV(st+1) − V(st), λ ≈ 0.95.
