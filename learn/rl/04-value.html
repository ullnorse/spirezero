Week 1 · Day 5

Value: how good is this situation?

You already have this instinct. Twelve HP, the Worm at 20 with Strength up, two Defends in hand: "this is bad". RL makes that feeling a number, and the number turns out to be the key to learning long chains of decisions.

The idea

The value of a state is the reward you expect to collect from here to the end of the fight, if you keep playing the way you play. With our +1/−1 reward, value is just (win probability − loss probability). Two things to hold onto:

  • Value depends on the policy. The same state is worth more to a good player than to a random one.
  • Value can be estimated by playing it out. Start from the state, finish the fight many times, average the results. That is a Monte Carlo estimate and it is exactly what you do when you say "I'd win this about 7 times in 10".

The value of an action in a state (written Q) is the same thing with the first move fixed: take this action, then play on as usual. Once you have Q for every legal action, the best move is simply the biggest one.

When Q disagrees with the policy that produced it, you have found an improvement. Act greedily on Q, and the policy gets better. Do that over and over and you have, in outline, most of classical RL.

Where this lives in SpireZero
  • Rollouts need a copy of the state to play forward from. The engine's Combat::clone_from and the no-allocation copy test (crates/sts2-core/tests/it/copy_alloc.rs) exist so search and rollouts are cheap; docs/perf.md section S4A′ is about exactly that.
  • cargo xtask bench --mode fights --lookahead: the "LA" workload is a one-step lookahead player, a tiny ancestor of the Q bars above, run as a speed benchmark.
  • In PPO (day 9) a neural net predicts V(s) from the observation; it is called the critic, and it plays the role of the rollouts here without having to run them.
The math, gently

The state-value function of a policy π:

Vπ(s) = E[ Gt | st = s, actions from π ]

and the action-value function:

Qπ(s, a) = E[ Gt | st = s, at = a, then π ]

The Monte Carlo estimate is the sample mean of returns over rollouts. The one relationship that everything else builds on is the Bellman equation: the value of now equals the reward now plus the value of what comes next,

Vπ(s) = E[ r + γ · Vπ(s′) ]

γ (gamma) is a discount in [0, 1] that makes far-off reward count a little less. It keeps infinite sums finite and nudges the agent to win sooner; our fights end, so γ close to 1 is fine. Day 6 turns this equation into a learning rule.
