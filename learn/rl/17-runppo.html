Week 4 · Day 17

PPO over a run

Day 10's loop, unchanged, over day 16's env: a net that sees the run and scores all 355 indices, a reward that has to be the floor because the victory never comes, and a training run that is measured by day 16's protocol. It climbs from random to the rule in a minute, and the page says what that does and does not prove.

The idea

Nothing in PPO cares whether a step is a card or a map node. What changes is the episode: long, with a reward that at this level is −1 for every run, so the advantage is noise and nothing learns. Reward the floors climbed instead and the objective becomes "get further", which is the honest objective until a victory exists to reward. GAE's γ moves toward 1 because the consequences of a card choice arrive many decisions later.

Run features

Ten scalars from the header (HP fraction, gold, floor, deck size, relic count, belt, map row, removals), a one-hot of the phase, then three multi-hots: the deck's card kinds, the card kinds offered on the rewards screen, and the relics. 1,443 floats. The offered-cards block is what makes a card-pick decision possible: without it, index 172 ("card 0 of reward 0") is a blind pick. Day 14's lesson applies again and is left for you: a slot-aligned head over the five offered cards with embeddings is the next structural step.

Read it with day 13's eyes. The floor-trained net beat random by two floors, seven standard errors, and matched the trivial rule within noise. In 60 iterations it rediscovered "take the cards": the training floor climbed from 12.4 to about 15 and the entropy fell from 1.45 to 0.8. Beating the rule is the next experiment, and the gap to look at is in the per-phase decisions: the rule rests at every rest site and picks the first card, and both are often wrong. The sparse net's rollout floor went down while it trained: a normalised constant reward is pure noise, and noise with a learning rate is a random walk.

What is different about runs

  • Length. A greedy-fought run is 25 to 60 agent decisions, 130 engine actions. With T = 64 most rollouts hold a run's end, so GAE still bootstraps from a real value often; with combat="agent" a run is several hundred decisions and T has to grow or γλ has to shrink.
  • Loops. A deck selection can be cancelled and reopened for ever. The floor reward gives a loop nothing, so PPO leaves it, but an argmax policy from a random net can fall into one; day 16's measure_floors caps its steps and day 18's "stalling episodes" row is the symptom.
  • Throughput. The greedy resolver plans every enemy turn on copies: 4,000 decisions a second on four threads against 300,000 fight steps. The update, not the env, is still what the GPU waits on, but the ratio is a hundred times tighter and the batch is small, so every iteration is a few seconds of real time.
  • Two policies in one run. combat="agent" hands the fight decisions to the same net through indices 0 to 139, with the combat observation at offset 1,750 of the run observation. The featuriser can route those bytes through day 14's slot net and the run bytes through this one, and the two heads share the value. That is the design the capstone does not do, and the first thing to try after it.

The math, gently

With every episode's return equal to −1, the advantage estimate At = Gt − V(st) is −1 − V(st) for every t once V has fitted, that is zero in expectation and pure estimation noise; normalising the batch rescales that noise to unit variance and the policy takes a random step each iteration. A reward of c per floor makes the return c · (floors still to come), which differs between actions, which is a gradient.

The floor reward is not potential-based: Φ = c · floor would give γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) = c(γ floor′ − floor), and at death the terminal Φ is 0, a penalty of −c · floor that the exercise deliberately leaves out so that a death on floor 15 is not worse than one on floor 5. The two objectives have different optima; the exercise's one is "get far", not "get far and die low".
